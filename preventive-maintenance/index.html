Who a program is for

Preventive maintenance, spelled preventative in plenty of maintenance departments, is the same thing: a planned service program with an agreed scope, sometimes set up as a maintenance agreement or a service contract. Two different people call us. One has a machine that has stopped and needs it running. The other is a maintenance or production manager who would rather not have that call at all, and wants the equipment fleet on a plan. This page is for the second one. The first route has not changed and is not affected by any of this.

What the agreement covers

Every program is written down before it starts. For your site the proposal states the agreed equipment and where it sits, the visit cadence for each machine, the planned production windows the visits run inside, the scope of work carried out at a visit, the written findings you receive afterwards, how open items are tracked and closed between visits, and how labour, travel, consumables and replacement parts are handled. Support terms, agreement duration and the payment schedule are set out in the same document. You approve that scope before the first visit, and the aim of running it is fewer avoidable interruptions and a clear, current picture of what your equipment is doing.

It starts with the equipment list

The first thing a program needs is an agreed list of what it covers: manufacturer, model and serial number, site and location on the line, configuration, and the role the machine plays in your operation. That list is the document everything else refers to, and it is the thing most often missing when a plant asks for maintenance “on the lasers”.

Alongside identity we record the starting condition of each machine. Without a baseline, a finding at the third visit has nothing to be compared against, and neither of us can tell a new problem from one that was always there.

Production criticality, on three questions

Not every machine deserves the same planning, and priority should be something you can check rather than something we assert. We classify each machine on three questions you can answer from your own floor:

  1. What stops when it stops. Does the line stop, does output drop, or does nothing downstream notice?
  2. Whether there is spare capacity. Is there a second machine, an alternative route, or none?
  3. How long the parts take. Days from a distributor, weeks from overseas, or a part that no longer exists and would have to be substituted or engineered.

A machine that stops a line, has no backup and carries long-lead or obsolete parts is planned differently from one with a spare sitting beside it. That is the whole point of the classification, and it is why the third question matters as much as the first on equipment whose manufacturer has moved on.

Two service programs

Both are real service cadences, not billing labels. A monthly program means a monthly visit. A quarterly program means a visit every three months. Which one fits is decided per machine, against its use and its manufacturer requirements, before anything is agreed.

What you receive after every visit

The deliverable is a record, not a signature on a sheet. For each covered machine, every visit produces:

  • The equipment and the visit identified, with the work completed under the agreed scope.
  • Current condition findings, and any limitation that affected the assessment.
  • Open items, their priority, and the follow-up work proposed for each.
  • Parts or corrective repairs that need a quotation or a decision from you.
  • The next planned service date or scheduling window.

Records carry between visits, so an open item raised in March is still an open item in June until it is closed or withdrawn. That continuity is what lets a purchasing team see what it is being asked to approve and why, and it is what separates completed maintenance from work that is still outstanding.

Coverage and responsibilities, stated before the program starts

The proposal names the covered machines and sites, the visit frequency, the planned scope of each visit, and what we need from you to carry it out. It states in writing how labour, travel, consumables and replacement parts are treated, because that is where maintenance agreements usually go wrong.

Repairs inside the coverage, and repairs beyond it

Corrective work can be part of the program, and the proposal says which work that is. Where it is included, it is carried out at the visit and recorded in the report. A fault outside the agreed coverage is recorded in the same report and quoted for your approval, so the invoice never widens inside a maintenance window without your agreement.

Planned downtime is agreed, not assumed

Before a visit we agree with your named contact which equipment is released, how access works and what service window is available. Findings that affect further work are recorded so your team can decide what to address inside the current window and what needs its own plan.

Written into the proposal, machine by machine

Covered equipment and sites. Visit cadence per machine. Planned production windows and the access we need. Scope of work at each visit. Written findings, and the open-item list carried forward until each item is closed. Responsibility for labour, travel, consumables and replacement parts. Support terms, duration and payment schedule. Everything agreed with your team sits in that one document, so your maintenance manager and your purchasing manager are reading the same commitments. Questions maintenance and purchasing teams ask

Is monthly or quarterly maintenance right for my machine?

It depends on the machine, not on a rule. Operating hours and shift pattern, the environment it runs in, its service history and the manufacturer’s own stated requirements all change the answer, and so does the downtime your operation can release. A monthly visit suits equipment under heavy use or in a critical position on the line. A quarterly visit suits equipment whose requirements and use fit that cadence, and facilities coordinating several machines around established shutdown windows. We will recommend one if you would rather we decided, and the proposal states the interval for each machine.

Does a monthly program mean every task happens every month?

No. A monthly program means a monthly service visit on an agreed scope. Individual tasks keep the intervals appropriate to the machine, so some are done at every visit and others less often. Equipment that requires attention more frequently than the program cadence keeps that requirement, and the program does not replace it.

If you find a fault during a maintenance visit, is the repair included?

Yes, when the corrective work is included in the agreed coverage: it is carried out at the visit and recorded in the report. Work that falls outside the agreed coverage is identified, recorded in the same report and quoted for your approval before it proceeds, so nothing unexpected reaches your invoice.

Do you still take one-off service calls?

Yes. A maintenance program is an addition, not a replacement. Machine-down calls, one-off evaluations, component-level repair and retrofit work continue exactly as before, whether or not you have a program.

Where do you run maintenance programs?

Across the United States and Canada. Parsippany, New Jersey is the base and the Northeast gets the shortest travel, but a scheduled visit anywhere is a matter of planning it far enough ahead. Travel is stated separately and clearly in the proposal.

Ask for a program

Whether you call it a preventive maintenance program, a preventative maintenance schedule, a maintenance agreement or a service contract, the request is the same. Tell us which machines you want covered, where they are, how they run, and whether you have monthly or quarterly planned maintenance visits in mind. If the interval is not clear yet, ask us to recommend one. Shutdown windows and any existing maintenance records help.
