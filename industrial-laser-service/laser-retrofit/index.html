What a retrofit is, and what it is not

A retrofit replaces a subsystem that can no longer be supported, inside a machine that is otherwise worth keeping. Typically that is the control chain: CNC and control hardware, HMI or industrial PC, servo drives and motor groups, I/O and the safety circuit. Sometimes it is the source. Sometimes it is only the interface between two things that were never designed to talk to each other.

What it is not is a rebuild dressed up, and it is not a way to sell a bigger job. A retrofit is the right answer when the mechanics and the structure are sound and the electronics are the constraint. When the frame, the rails or the optics are the problem, a retrofit puts new electronics on a machine that will still not hold tolerance, and we will say that rather than take the order.

Repair, retrofit, or replace

Three outcomes. Which one applies to your machine is a finding rather than a preference, and we put the comparison in writing, with the cost of each route stated, so a maintenance manager and a purchasing manager are reading the same document.

SituationThe answer that fitsWhy
A board, drive or supply has failed and parts existRepair Component-level work restores the machine without changing anything else, and it is the least invasive route.
The control platform, HMI or drive family is out of productionRetrofit the chain Replacing one obsolete part at a time produces a machine nobody can support. Replacing the chain as a group gives a documented, obtainable configuration.
The source is at end of life on a sound machineSource replacement or conversion The frame, motion and handling are the expensive parts and they are still good.
Frame, rails, way surfaces or structure are worn outReplace the machine New electronics on tired mechanics does not produce parts in tolerance. We will tell you this plainly.

Which row applies to your machine is a finding, not a sales position, and it is stated in writing before you approve anything.

Why we replace the whole control chain

When electronics are the constraint, the usual failure of a repair-only approach is that it leaves a machine made of parts from four different eras, where each replacement narrows what the next one can be. Six months later the next failure has no solution at all.

So where a retrofit is the answer, we replace the chain rather than one part: CNC and control system, servo drives and complete motor groups, the operator interface, the I/O and safety circuit, and the cutting head and source where those are part of the constraint. The result is a machine whose configuration is documented, whose parts are currently available, and which the shop can support without going back to a defunct builder. The documentation is delivered with the machine.

CO2 to fiber, and when not to do it

Converting a CO2 metal-cutting machine to a fiber source is a real option and it is not automatic. It works when the frame and motion suit the speeds a fiber source makes possible, when the head, delivery and chilling can be brought to match, and when the control platform can be brought along or replaced in the same project. It does not work when any of those is the real limit, and it never makes sense simply because fiber is newer.

It also does not follow that a CO2 machine should be converted at all. On non-metals, CO2 is frequently the correct process and converting it would be a downgrade. Our CO2 service scope is current work, so the choice here is genuinely a choice and not a route to a bigger invoice.

What brings owners to a retrofit

These are the descriptions we are given. Each one is produced by more than one cause, so none of them identifies a fault on its own. They are the right way to start a request, and every one of them is something you can see without opening anything.

  • Control system obsolete and the mechanics are still sound
  • HMI or industrial PC failed and no replacement is available
  • Drives or motors out of production with no drop-in equivalent
  • Original CO2 source at end of life on a good frame
  • Machine builder gone, dissolved or no longer supporting the model
  • Software licence or dongle lost with no route to a replacement
  • Safety circuit or interlocks non-compliant with current practice
  • Spare parts only available used, at unpredictable prices

Machines whose builder, dealer or software vendor has gone

A frequent retrofit case is not a broken machine but an unsupportable one. A builder that dissolved, a dealer that stopped trading, a control platform whose vendor has moved on, a licence or dongle that cannot be replaced, or a configuration nobody recorded. The mechanics are fine. The route to keeping it running is gone.

That is a documentation and configuration problem as much as a hardware one, and it is work we do: establishing what the machine actually is, what its interfaces need, and what a supportable configuration looks like, then building that and handing over the records. Pinouts, measured operating values, interface definitions and as-found configuration are delivered with the machine, because the whole point is that you are not dependent on us afterwards either.

How a job runs

  1. What you send. Machine make, model and serial number, the laser source model if you have it, the control platform on the operator panel, what the machine does now and what changed. Photographs of labels and of the fault display help.
  2. Written scope and a fixed evaluation fee. Quoted before anyone travels, with what the evaluation covers, the expected turnaround and what a negative result would mean.
  3. What the evaluation covers. The as-found condition, the actual configuration rather than the recorded one, the machine and the laser as separable systems, and the services around them, with instrumentation named in the proposal.
  4. Written findings. What was measured, what was found, which subsystem the fault sits in, what remains unknown, and the options with their cost and risks.
  5. Your approval. Nothing is repaired, substituted or modified until you approve the scope and the price in writing.
  6. Work, validation and report. Inside the approved scope, then validation that the agreed scope was met, and a written service report you keep.

Questions owners ask

Is a retrofit worth it on an old machine?

It depends on which part is old. Sound mechanics with dead electronics is the case a retrofit is for. Worn frame, rails or way surfaces is not, and we will tell you that rather than sell you new electronics for a machine that will still miss tolerance. The comparison between repair, retrofit and replacement is put in writing.

Why replace the whole control chain instead of the one part that failed?

Because replacing obsolete parts one at a time produces a machine made of four eras, where every repair narrows the next one. Replacing the chain as a group gives a configuration that is documented and currently obtainable, and the documentation is handed over with it.

Can you convert a CO2 machine to fiber?

Sometimes, and sometimes you should not. It works when the frame, motion, head, delivery and chilling suit it and the control platform can come along. On non-metals CO2 is often the correct process and a conversion would be a downgrade. We service CO2 as current work, so this is a real choice rather than a sales route.

Our machine builder no longer exists. Can the machine be kept running?

Usually yes. What has gone is the documentation, parts and support route, not the machine. Establishing what the machine is and what a supportable configuration looks like is part of the work, and the records are delivered to you.

Do you do the retrofit on site or off?

It depends on the scope and on the downtime your operation can release. The proposal states which, what access is needed and how the planned downtime is coordinated, before anything starts.
